One question. Five possible meanings.

Will This Count?

Maybe. But first we need to know what you mean by count. A course can transfer for credit and still not satisfy the particular GE, major, course, or degree requirement you have in mind.

The one idea to remember

“Transferable” is useful information. It is not the end of the question.

Transferable ≠ automatically counts for everything

Choose what you mean

When you say “count,” which question are you asking?

Select one. The page will give you the short answer, what it means, and where you should verify it.

Where to check
Do not assume

See the layers

One course can have several different transfer relationships.

These relationships can overlap. The important move is to ask which relationship answers the question in front of you.

Transfer credit: “Will the units transfer?”

A transferable course can earn credit at a receiving institution. For UC, approved CCC transferable courses are listed through the UC Transfer Course Agreement information available in ASSIST. UC also explains that a transferable course may earn elective credit even when it is not equivalent to one particular UC course.

Translation: getting transfer credit does not automatically tell you how that credit will apply to GE, a major, or a specific course requirement.

General education: “Will this satisfy a GE area?”

General education is a separate question from basic transferability. A course may be transferable but not approved for the GE area a student needs.

Translation: check the current GE pattern or agreement that applies to the student rather than relying only on the word “transferable.”

Major preparation: “Will this help me enter or progress in my major?”

Major-preparation agreements identify community college coursework that may satisfy preparation requirements for a particular major or program at a receiving institution. UC explicitly distinguishes these agreements from general education and course-by-course articulation.

Translation: completing GE does not necessarily mean major preparation is complete.

Course-to-course articulation: “Does this replace that university course?”

ASSIST defines articulation agreements as formal agreements between institutions that explain how coursework at one institution may satisfy requirements at another. Department and prefix agreements can show course-to-course or series articulation.

Translation: this is the question to ask when you mean, “Does my CCC course stand in for this specific university course?”

Degree or pathway use: “Does it fit the program I am completing here?”

A course can also satisfy a requirement in a local associate degree, an Associate Degree for Transfer, or another college program. That is a different question from whether the receiving university gives transfer credit or uses it for a specific major.

Translation: the same course can play one role at the community college and a different role after transfer.

A three-step habit

Before you say “yes, it counts,” do this.

This tiny checking habit prevents a lot of transfer misunderstandings.

Step 1 · Name the question

What kind of “count”?

Transfer credit? GE? Major prep? A specific university course? A degree or pathway requirement?

Step 2 · Name the destination

For which institution and major?

Transfer relationships can depend on the receiving institution, campus, major, and academic year.

Step 3 · Verify

Use the current official source.

For California articulation, start with ASSIST. For individualized planning, confirm with the appropriate counselor or college professional.

Use the idea differently

Student and faculty versions of the same skill

If you're a student

You do not have to decode every transfer rule yourself. Your job is to get specific enough to ask the right question.

  • “Will this course transfer for credit?”
  • “Does it satisfy my GE pattern?”
  • “Does it satisfy major preparation for this campus and major?”
  • “Is there an articulation agreement for this course?”

If you're faculty

You can be genuinely helpful without taking on the role of an academic counselor.

  • Explain the difference among transferability, GE, articulation, and major preparation.
  • Show students where current information lives.
  • Avoid making individualized promises from memory.
  • Refer complete educational-plan questions to counseling or the appropriate campus professional.
Why the academic year matters ASSIST notes that institutions publish and update agreements on different timelines. Always look at the academic year that is relevant to the question, and do not assume an older agreement tells you what is current.
