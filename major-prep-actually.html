The other half of transfer preparation

Major Prep, Actually.

General education helps you transfer as a broadly educated student. Major preparation helps you arrive ready for the specific program you want to enter.

The one idea to remember

Two students can both be “done with GE” and be in very different positions for transfer if one has completed the courses expected for the intended major and the other has not.

GE answers: “What broad learning do I need?”
Major prep answers: “What does this major expect before I arrive?”

Do not mix these up

Cal-GETC and major preparation solve different problems.

You often need both. The amount of attention each deserves depends on the student's major and destination.

General education

Cal-GETC

  • Broad lower-division general education
  • Organized into statewide subject areas
  • Can help satisfy lower-division GE after transfer
  • Does not, by itself, tell you what a particular major expects
Major-specific

Major preparation

  • Courses tied to the intended field of study
  • Can differ by university, campus, school, and major
  • May include required and recommended coursework
  • Can affect admission competitiveness and progress after transfer

The trap: “I finished my GE” can sound like “I finished everything I need before transfer.” Those are not the same sentence.

The four-question habit

Major prep only makes sense after you name the destination.

Before looking for courses, pin down these four things.

1

What major?

“Biology” and “Biochemistry” may have different preparation even at the same university.

2

Which university?

The same major can have different preparation across CSU, UC, and participating independent institutions.

3

Which campus/program?

A campus, college, school, or impacted program may add its own expectations.

4

Which academic year?

Articulation and requirements can change. Match the plan to the correct current agreement or guidance.

What deserves priority?

Some majors make major preparation especially urgent.

There is no universal rule that says “finish all GE first.” The balance depends on the program.

High-prep majors

Engineering, many sciences, and other sequential programs

Courses can build on one another in long sequences. Missing an early calculus, chemistry, physics, or prerequisite course can delay later coursework.

Keep options open

Students considering several campuses

Compare preparation across destinations before assuming one campus's list will work everywhere. UC Transfer Pathways can simplify preparation for selected UC majors.

CSU

Impacted campuses and majors

A CSU campus or program with more qualified applicants than available space may use higher or additional admission standards. Current campus/program information matters.

Faculty

Courses that sit inside transfer pathways

A course revision may affect articulation or how the course serves a transfer major. Faculty should involve the articulation officer rather than assuming a curricular change is transfer-neutral.

Where the answer lives

For a specific campus and major, start with the major agreement in ASSIST.

ASSIST major agreements show university major requirements and the community-college courses that may be used to satisfy those lower-division requirements. They may also include advisories or admission notes.

1. Open the right academic year

Requirements and articulation can change. Do not automatically use the newest year if a different year applies to the student's planning context.

2. Choose “View Agreement by Major”

When a major agreement is published, this is the clearest place to see lower-division preparation for that program.

3. Read every note

Required, recommended, admissions, grade, sequence, and selection notes can matter as much as the course-to-course rows.

Useful habit: do not stop when you find one matching course. Read the entire major agreement as a plan. Practice reading an agreement in “ASSIST, Actually.”

Transfer language

Required, recommended, and articulated are not synonyms.

Open the terms you need. These tiny distinctions can change how a student prioritizes coursework.

Required preparation

Coursework or conditions a program identifies as required for admission, selection, or preparation. The exact consequence of missing a requirement depends on the institution and program, so verify the current wording.

Recommended preparation

Coursework a program strongly encourages but does not label the same way as a required course. “Recommended” should not automatically be read as “irrelevant,” especially in selective or highly sequential majors.

Articulated course

An official agreement identifies how coursework at the sending institution may satisfy a receiving-institution course or requirement. Articulation answers a different question from whether the receiving program requires that course in the first place.

No course articulated

ASSIST explains that this means no established articulation is displayed for that university course and community college in the agreement. It does not automatically mean the CCC course is nontransferable.

Know your major but not your UC campus?

UC Transfer Pathways provide a single set of major-preparation courses for selected popular majors across participating UC campuses. They can help students keep multiple UC options open.

What do I do with this?

Students and faculty have different jobs.

Student

Build the plan backward from the destination.

  • Name the intended major.
  • Choose realistic campuses to compare.
  • Check the correct ASSIST major agreements.
  • Notice required, recommended, grade, sequence, and admissions notes.
  • Review the full plan with counseling rather than planning from one webpage in isolation.
Faculty

Understand the pathway without becoming the student's counselor.

  • Know whether your course appears in major-preparation pathways.
  • Understand how articulation differs from transferability.
  • Loop in articulation when revising transfer-relevant courses.
  • Help students identify what to verify rather than guessing individualized requirements.
