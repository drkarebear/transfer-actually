UC transfer application writing

PIQs, Actually.

A Personal Insight Question is not a five-paragraph English essay. UC is asking for useful information about you: what you did, how you think, how you prepared, and what your experiences help the reader understand.

The current transfer basics

1 required question
Choose 3 of 7 others
350 words maximum each
All 7 optional prompts are equal

You do not get extra points for choosing a supposedly “harder” optional prompt.

Change the mental model

Think evidence, not performance.

The strongest response is not necessarily the most dramatic or the most polished-sounding. It is the one that answers the question specifically and helps UC understand the applicant.

A PIQ can be direct.

You can answer the question in the first few sentences. You do not need a mysterious hook before the reader learns what the response is about.

A PIQ is not a résumé paragraph.

Listing awards, jobs, activities, or responsibilities without showing what you actually did gives the reader less insight.

“I” is useful.

UC explicitly encourages first-person statements. The reader needs to know your decisions, actions, contributions, and learning.

You do not need a hardship story.

Challenge is only one optional topic. Leadership, creativity, skill, educational opportunity, community contribution, and other strengths can be equally useful.

The required transfer PIQ

Show how you prepared for the major and upper-division work.

UC's required transfer question asks about preparation for the intended major and readiness to succeed after transfer. That means the response should do more than explain why the subject is interesting.

1. Major

What are you preparing to study?

2. Preparation

Which courses, projects, jobs, research, service, or experiences built that preparation?

3. Evidence

What did you actually do, solve, make, analyze, practice, or learn?

4. Readiness

What skills or habits now make upper-division work more realistic?

5. Direction

How does this preparation connect to what you want to do next?

Useful test: if the response could be written by almost anyone who likes the same major, it probably needs more specific evidence from your experience.

Strong PIQ moves

Give the reader something concrete to learn about you.

Answer early.

Do not spend half the response setting the scene before addressing the prompt.

Use a specific example.

“I became a better leader” is a claim. A moment where you organized, decided, resolved, taught, or changed something is evidence.

Make your role visible.

If other people were involved, clarify what you contributed. This is why “I” statements matter.

Explain the effect.

What changed because of your actions? What did you understand differently? What skill did you build?

Let each PIQ add something.

Four responses that all prove the same quality waste space. Use the set to show different dimensions of your preparation and experience.

Sound like yourself.

Clear and conversational is better than replacing your natural language with words you would never actually use.

Usually weaker

  • 150 words of background before your actions become clear.
  • A list of accomplishments with no example or reflection.
  • Writing primarily about a parent, teacher, boss, or friend instead of your own role.
  • Trying to make every response inspirational.
  • Choosing a prompt because you think UC “likes it more.” UC says the optional questions receive equal consideration.
  • Making the required major-preparation PIQ mostly about “I have always loved…” without showing preparation.
  • Editing so heavily that the final answer no longer sounds like the student who lived the experience.

Choosing the three optional PIQs

Choose the questions that reveal the best evidence you actually have.

UC says the seven optional questions are considered equally. The goal is not to predict a secret preferred prompt.

Leadership

Where did you influence, organize, mentor, resolve, or take responsibility?

A title is not required. Family, work, school, and community leadership can all provide evidence.

Creativity

How do you make, solve, design, adapt, or think originally?

Creativity can be artistic, technical, practical, entrepreneurial, academic, or problem-solving.

Talent or skill

What have you deliberately developed over time?

Recognition or awards are not required. Development and demonstration matter.

Educational opportunity / barrier

What opportunity did you use well, or what educational barrier did you actively navigate?

Focus on your choices, strategy, resourcefulness, and what the experience changed.

Challenge + academics

What significant challenge affected you academically, and what steps did you take?

The academic connection matters. Do not assume the challenge alone tells the reader what you did.

Community

What did you do to improve a group, school, workplace, family, or community you belong to?

Define the community, then make your action and contribution visible.

What else?

What important strength or context has the rest of the application not shown?

Use this to add something meaningful, not merely repeat information already clear elsewhere.

Across all three

Which combination gives the reader the clearest picture of you?

Choose for evidence and range, not because one prompt sounds more impressive.

AI and feedback: keep the student as the author.

UC's current transfer guidance emphasizes authentic voice and personal experience. UC also says feedback from family, teachers, and friends can be useful, but the answers should reflect the applicant's own ideas and words.

Current UC counselor materials go a step further on AI: AI tools may be useful in early brainstorming, but the final PIQ should be entirely the student's own in voice, substance, and reflection.

A safer use of AI: ask for brainstorming questions, ask where a draft is vague, or ask which sentences lack concrete evidence. A poor use: asking AI to write or rewrite the PIQ into a polished admissions voice that is no longer yours.

Before you paste the PIQ into the application

  • I answered the actual question.
  • A reader can identify what I did.
  • I used at least one concrete example rather than only general claims.
  • I explained why the example matters or what it shows.
  • The four PIQs do not all tell the same story.
  • The wording still sounds like me.
  • The response is my own work and uses my own words.
  • I proofread for clarity, spelling, and accidental formatting problems.
  • I saved a clean copy before pasting it into the application.
